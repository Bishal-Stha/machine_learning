1. What is NLP, and what are its main goals & challenges?

Foundations

Definition: Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the area of AI that enables computers to read, understand, and generate human language. It blends linguistics and machine learning.

Primary goals: text understanding (classification, NER), generation (summarization, dialogue), retrieval (search), and reasoning (QA).

Main challenges include:

  • Ambiguity — lexical ("bank") and syntactic (attachment ambiguities).
  • Context — meaning depends on previous sentences, world knowledge.
  • Figurative language — idioms, sarcasm, metaphors.
  • Domain & language drift — models break when deployed in different domains or time periods.

Teaching tip: show the same short sentence in several contexts to reveal ambiguity (e.g., "I saw her duck").

2. Syntax vs Semantics vs Pragmatics

Linguistic levels

Syntax = structure/grammar. Example: parse trees, dependency arcs.
Semantics = meaning of words and compositions (who did what to whom).

Pragmatics = meaning in usage; intent, implicature, politeness. Example: "Can you pass the salt?" is a request, not a yes/no question.

Teaching tip: draw a sentence tree for syntax, show sentence paraphrases for semantics, and role-play pragmatic contexts.

3. Tokenization — why it matters

Preprocessing

Tokenization splits text into atomic units (tokens). Types:

  • Word-level — common but fails with unknown words.
  • Subword (BPE, WordPiece) — handles rare words and morphology.
  • Character — robust but longer sequences.

Modern NLP uses subword tokenizers to strike balance between vocabulary size and flexibility. Tokens map to IDs and then to embeddings.

Teaching tip: show how a new word like "chatgptish" gets split by BPE into familiar subwords.

4. Text → Numbers: IDs and Embeddings

Representation

Pipeline: token → integer ID → embedding vector. Embeddings are dense floating-point vectors (e.g., 768-d) learned so semantically similar tokens are nearby in vector space.

Operation examples: cosine similarity for semantic distance; vector arithmetic ("king" - "man" + "woman" ≈ "queen").

Teaching tip: show a small 2D scatter of words clustered by topic (animals, fruits).

5. What are embeddings and how they represent meaning?

Embeddings

Definition: Embeddings are learned dense vectors that encode semantic/usage information. They derive from the distributional hypothesis: words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings.

Properties:

  • Capture synonyms and relatedness (proximity in space).
  • Support algebraic relationships (analogies).
  • Enable downstream models to start from meaningful representations.

Teaching tip: demonstrate cosine similarity between pairs like ("dog","cat") vs ("dog","table").

6. Word2Vec: CBOW vs Skip-gram (intuition)

Shallow embedding learners

CBOW predicts a target word from surrounding context tokens. Good when you have lots of data and want speed.

Skip-gram predicts surrounding words from a target token. Better for infrequent words and capturing rare-context signals.

Teaching tip: sketch a sliding window and show arrows either from context→target (CBOW) or target→context (skip-gram).

7. Limitations of static embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe)

Why context matters

Static embeddings assign a single vector per word type. This fails for polysemous words like "bank" (river vs finance). They also cannot model context-specific nuances or word sense shifts across domains.

Teaching tip: show the same word used in two sentences and explain why a single vector cannot capture both meanings well.

8. Contextual embeddings (ELMo, BERT, GPT) — the fix

Contextualization

Contextual embeddings are computed per token instance and change depending on surrounding tokens. BERT uses bidirectional context (left+right), while GPT-style models use left-to-right context for generation.

Benefits: handle polysemy, adapt to syntax and nearby discourse, improve downstream performance.

9. Positional encoding — why Transformers need it

Order information

Transformers process tokens in parallel and therefore need a way to encode order. Positional encodings (sine/cosine or learned vectors) are added to token embeddings so the model can reason about relative and absolute positions.

PE(pos,2i)=sin(pos/10000^{2i/d}), PE(pos,2i+1)=cos(...) is a common formula used in the original Transformer paper.

10. RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs — sequence models

Sequential processing

RNNs maintain a hidden state as they read tokens sequentially. They suffer from vanishing gradients for long-range dependencies. LSTMs and GRUs use gating (forget, input, output) to preserve or discard information and capture longer dependencies.

Teaching tip: show a long sentence where a verb at the end depends on an early subject — LSTMs handle such cases better than vanilla RNNs.

11. Attention mechanism — intuition & math

Core idea

Intuition: when producing an output (e.g., translating a word), the model 'attends' more to input tokens that are relevant.

Mechanics: transform inputs into Query (Q), Key (K), Value (V) vectors. Compute scores as QK^T / sqrt(d_k), apply softmax to get weights, then weighted-sum over V.

Teaching tip: walk through a toy example of three tokens where one token strongly matches the query and dominates the weighted sum.

12. Transformer architecture & self-attention

Modern backbone

Transformers stack layers of multi-head self-attention and position-wise feed-forward networks with residual connections and layer normalization. Benefits:

  • Parallel computation (faster on modern hardware)
  • Direct long-range token interactions via attention
  • Flexible stacking into encoder/decoder variants

Teaching tip: use the diagram to point out encoder-only vs decoder-only vs encoder-decoder flow.

13. Encoder, Decoder, Encoder–Decoder: differences

Model families

Encoder-only (e.g., BERT): excel at understanding tasks (classification, NER). Trained with masked tokens to leverage bidirectional context.

Decoder-only (e.g., GPT): autoregressive generators that predict the next token given previous tokens. Great for generation, completion, and in-context learning.

Encoder–Decoder (e.g., T5, BART): map input sequences to output sequences (translation, summarization). Combine understanding and generation.

14. Language modeling — predicting the next word

Probabilistic modeling

A language model estimates probabilities of token sequences: P(w1..wn)=∏P(wi|w1..w(i-1)). Autoregressive models directly model the conditional probabilities used for sampling/generation. Perplexity is a common measure that summarizes how well the model predicts held-out text (lower is better).

15. Pretraining objectives: Masked LM vs Causal LM

Self-supervision

Masked Language Modeling (MLM) — randomly mask tokens and predict them from both left and right context (BERT). Good for representation learning and downstream classification.
Causal Language Modeling (CLM) — predict next token from past tokens only (GPT). Naturally suited for generation.

Teaching tip: show masked input and what the model sees during training vs what it sees at inference.

16. Transfer learning & fine-tuning

Practical adaptation

Large models are pretrained on broad corpora and then fine-tuned on specific tasks using labeled data. Fine-tuning typically adds a small task-specific head and updates weights with a smaller learning rate and fewer epochs.

Teaching tip: compare training from scratch vs fine-tuning on a small dataset to show efficiency gains.

17. Why LLMs can perform unseen tasks: in‑context learning

Emergence

Large models learn many patterns during pretraining. Given a prompt that includes examples or an instruction, they can infer the task and produce correct outputs without parameter updates — this is in-context learning. Few-shot prompting places a few labeled examples in the prompt; zero-shot uses only instructions.

Teaching tip: show a few-shot prompt for translation or classification and then a new input; demonstrate the model generalizing.

18. Evaluation metrics for NLP

How we measure

Classification: accuracy, precision, recall, F1. Sequence labeling: token-level F1. Generation: BLEU (translation), ROUGE (summarization), METEOR; BERTScore (semantic overlap).

Language modeling: perplexity. Keep in mind automatic metrics are imperfect for open-ended generation — human evaluation is often necessary.

19. Why high accuracy can still fail in the real world

Robustness & generalization

Test metrics can be gamed by dataset artifacts and spurious correlations. Models might exploit shortcuts (e.g., keyword triggers) and fail on distribution shifts, adversarial examples, or subtle reasoning tasks like sarcasm.

Teaching tip: present adversarial or out-of-domain examples that break a high‑accuracy model.

20. Ethical & reliability challenges in NLP

Safety & fairness

Major concerns:

  • Bias & fairness: models reflect societal biases present in training data (gender, race, etc.).
  • Hallucinations: confident but incorrect outputs, especially in open-ended generation.
  • Privacy: memorization of sensitive data during pretraining.
  • Explainability: hard to audit reasons for specific outputs.

Mitigations include careful data curation, fairness-aware training, retrieval-augmented generation for factual grounding, and human-in-the-loop evaluation.
